Experience and Projects

Lipad, electric vertical takeoff and landing quadplane

Personal design project, in progress

Lipad, Tagalog for fly, is my own lift-plus-cruise electric vertical takeoff and landing quadplane with four fixed lift motors and a rear pusher propeller. I chose its airfoil from an aerodynamics study, set a weight budget for every part, and I am modeling it part by part in SOLIDWORKS so the practice matches real design work. Fixed lift motors keep the design simpler than tilting mechanisms and easier to test at small scale. It is my main personal project and the center of my Midpoint Career Exploration Mind Map.

NASA alignment: Lipad matches the work of NASA Aerospace Technologists in flight structures and vehicle design. Its vertical lift and cruise layout raises the same questions the Aeronautics Research Mission Directorate studies in Revolutionary Vertical Lift Technology. Building it trains the design, structures, and test skills that Advanced Air Mobility employers such as Joby Aviation and Archer hire for.

Solar boat, CCSF Engineering Club

Team project, 2nd place overall, SMUD California Solar Regatta

With the City College of San Francisco Engineering Club, I helped build the frame that mounted the solar panel on our solar-powered boat. Our team placed second overall at the SMUD California Solar Regatta. The work was hands-on structural building: fitting parts, keeping the frame light, and fixing problems against a deadline. It was my first team engineering competition result.

NASA alignment: The project built the structural and teamwork skills that sit under aerospace structures work. Competing against other schools also showed me how judges score a working design and not just a drawing. It runs on the same build, test, and improve cycle used in NASA design projects.

Roboreturn, autonomous tennis ball launching robot

Introduction to Engineering final team project, Fall 2026, in progress

Roboreturn is my Introduction to Engineering final team project: an autonomous robot that rides a 24 foot beam and launches tennis balls at targets 18 and 22 feet to the side. No remote control is allowed, so our team used MATLAB to calculate launch speed for each distance before building. I worked on the launcher design, a side-firing layout that fits inside an 18 inch cube. The project closes with timed runs, a written report with MATLAB projectile plots, and a group presentation.

NASA alignment: Roboreturn is flight test thinking at small scale: predict the flight with math, build the machine, then measure where the real launches land. That predict, test, and compare loop is the core of flight test engineering at centers such as NASA Armstrong Flight Research Center.

The NASA equivalent is an Aerospace Technologist in the 0861 series, in flight structures or vehicle design, at centers such as NASA Ames Research Center for vertical lift and NASA Langley Research Center for structures and materials. My path is to finish at City College of San Francisco, where my grade point average is 3.52, then transfer in 2028 to an ABET-accredited aerospace engineering program at the University of California, Davis or San Jose State University. From there I will apply for internships through the NASA Office of STEM Engagement and then the Pathways program, which hires new engineers at the GS-7 level and can lead to a full-time position. The skills I am adding next are aerodynamics, structural analysis, finite element analysis, and composite materials, plus Python and Arduino coding for test data and aircraft systems. Long term, I plan a master degree in aircraft and spacecraft design and structures, with the Massachusetts Institute of Technology as my goal.
